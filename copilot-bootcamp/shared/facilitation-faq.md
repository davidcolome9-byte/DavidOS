# Facilitation FAQ: Handling Resistance, Low Adoption, and Hard Questions

This is for you, not for participants.

Most of what goes wrong in a boot camp like this is not about the tool. It is about what people think the tool means for them. This file covers the conversations that decide whether your program works.

## The one principle underneath all of this

Do not argue people into adoption. Nobody has ever been convinced by a facilitator winning a debate about AI in a Teams call.

What moves people is watching a peer they respect do something useful and thinking "I could do that." Your job is mostly to create the conditions for that and then get out of the way. When you find yourself constructing a persuasive counterargument, you have already lost the room you were trying to persuade.

---

# Part one: the skeptics

There are four different people who all look like skeptics from the front of the room. They need completely different responses, and giving the wrong one makes things worse.

## The person who tried it and got a bad result

**What it sounds like:** "I tried it. It gave me something useless."

**What is actually going on:** they are right. Their first prompt was probably three words and the output was probably a generic paragraph. They ran a fair test and it failed.

**What to do:** believe them immediately. Then ask to see the prompt. Nine times out of ten you can fix it in front of them in twenty seconds by adding a shape instruction, and that twenty seconds converts a skeptic into someone who tells other people about it.

**What not to do:** explain that they need to prompt better. That is the same sentence as "you used it wrong," and it makes people defensive about a tool they already do not like.

**What to say:** "Show me what you typed. I bet I know what happened."

## The person protecting a standard

**What it sounds like:** "I am not putting anything in front of a member that a computer wrote." Or: "Our case notes have to be defensible. This is not that."

**What is actually going on:** this person has a real professional standard and they are defending it. They are usually one of your best people. Treat this as the asset it is.

**What to do:** agree with them, out loud, in front of everyone. Then show them Week 4's audit prompt, the one that lists every claim in a draft the source does not support. Their objection is exactly right and the correct response to it is a control, which you have.

**What not to do:** reassure them that it is fine. It is not automatically fine, and telling a careful person to relax marks you as someone who does not take the work seriously.

**What to say:** "You are right and I am not going to talk you out of that. Nothing goes to a member that a person did not read and own. What I want to show you is a way to catch things in your own drafts that you would not catch by rereading."

## The person who has seen initiatives come and go

**What it sounds like:** "We did something like this two years ago." Said flatly, usually not hostile.

**What is actually going on:** they have watched enthusiasm arrive and leave several times. They are not wrong about the pattern and they are protecting their own time.

**What to do:** do not defend the program. Acknowledge the pattern and describe what makes this one survive or not, honestly. Then make a small commitment and keep it. Say you will report at 30 days what is still in use, including the parts that died. Then actually do it.

**What not to do:** enthusiasm. Enthusiasm is the exact signal that confirms their prediction.

**What to say:** "Fair. Most of them do not stick. The thing that decides it is whether anybody is still using it in October, and I am going to report that either way, including if the answer is nobody."

## The person who is scared

**What it sounds like:** technical objections that keep moving. You answer one and a different one arrives. Or unusually aggressive questions about accuracy from someone who has not tried it.

**What is actually going on:** this is not a skeptic. This is somebody worried about their job, their competence, or being left behind, and the objections are cover. Answering the objection never works because it is not the real question.

**What to do:** stop answering objections. Talk to them privately, not in the room. Ask what they think this changes for their work. Then listen without rebutting.

**What not to do:** win the technical argument in front of the group. You will, and it will cost you that person permanently.

**What to say, privately:** "Can I ask what you think this means for how you work? I am not trying to sell you anything, I just want to know what you are seeing."

# Part two: "AI is going to replace us"

This is the hardest one and it deserves a straight answer rather than a reassuring one. People can tell the difference and the reassuring version costs you credibility.

## What not to say

**"It is just a tool."** Technically true, and it sounds like a brush off. Every technology that changed how work is done was just a tool.

**"It will free you up for higher value work."** People have heard this before, sometimes shortly before a reorganization. It is the single most distrusted sentence in corporate communication and using it marks you as someone repeating a line.

**"That will never happen here."** You do not know that, and if you are wrong, everyone will remember that you said it.

**"You should be more worried about not using it."** True in a narrow sense and it is a threat. Threats do not produce adoption, they produce quiet compliance and no real use.

## What to actually say

Be specific about what changes and what does not, and be honest that you do not control the outcome.

> "Here is what I think is true. This changes what a first draft costs. It does not change whether the work needs judgment. Deciding what a member does not need to be told, deciding whether the evidence supports a determination, deciding whether a number is worth escalating, none of that got easier this year.
>
> What I do not know is what leadership decides to do with time that gets freed up. I do not control that and neither do you, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.
>
> What I can tell you is that being the person who understands this well is a better position than being the person who avoided it, in every version of the future I can picture. That is why I am running this."

Then stop. Do not add a reassuring sentence at the end. The absence of a reassuring ending is what makes people believe the rest.

## If someone pushes further

**"So you are admitting jobs could go away."**

> "I am admitting I do not know. I would rather tell you that than tell you something comforting that I cannot back up. What I am confident about is the part I can control, which is that the people in this room will be good at this."

**"Then why should I help make it better?"**

Real question, deserves a real answer.

> "Because the alternative is that decisions get made about how this gets used by people who have never taken a fraud call. If the people who know the work do not shape how it gets used, somebody else will."

## The version for someone with a fraud or investigations background

This lands differently with people whose careers were built on judgment.

> "Everything you learned reading a case, noticing that a story does not hang together, knowing which question to ask third instead of first, none of that is in the tool. What the tool does is write the first version of the note faster. The part where you decide whether the evidence supports what the note says is still the whole job, and it is the part nobody has automated."

# Part three: low adoption

## Diagnose before you intervene

Low adoption has four causes and they need opposite responses. Guessing wrong wastes the intervention.

| Cause | Signal |
|---|---|
| Access | People ask where to find it |
| Skill | People tried once, stopped |
| Relevance | People cannot name a use |
| Permission | People ask if they are allowed |

**Access.** Somebody does not have a license, cannot find the entry point, or hit a login wall and gave up. This looks like resistance and is not. Fix it individually. It takes one message and it is the highest return thing on this list.

**Skill.** They tried, got a mediocre result, and concluded it was not for them. This is Week 3's whole subject. Fix it with a two minute personal demo using their own task, not with more training.

**Relevance.** They genuinely cannot see where it fits their work. Sometimes they are right, and if so, say so. More often nobody has connected it to something they actually do. Fix it by asking what they did last Tuesday that they did not enjoy.

**Permission.** They are not sure they are allowed, especially around member information, so they avoid the whole thing. Extremely common in regulated environments and almost never voiced. Fix it by getting a clear answer from compliance and stating it plainly and repeatedly.

## What to do when attendance drops in week 3 or 4

Normal. Do not respond by writing longer announcements or adding urgency, which reads as pleading and lowers attendance further.

Do this instead:

1. Ask two people who came what got them there. Use their words next time.
2. Ask one person who stopped coming what happened. Ask privately, ask once, and accept the answer.
3. Cut the cohort mentally to the people who are engaged and run a good session for them. A strong session with eight people produces more adoption than a limp one with sixteen.

## What to do when nobody submits

Submissions are a leading indicator and worth protecting.

Most common cause: the submission feels like homework being graded. Fix the framing, not the requirement. Say plainly that partial work counts and that a documented failure is a valid submission and more useful to the group than a clean success.

Second most common cause: no visible response to submissions. If people post and nothing happens, they stop posting. Reply to every submission, briefly, within 48 hours. One specific sentence is enough. This is tedious and it is the whole mechanism.

## What to do when it works for two weeks and then dies

This is the normal outcome of most training and it has one cause: no trigger.

"I will use Copilot more" dies in about nine days. "Every Friday when I build the weekly update I paste the numbers into this prompt" survives, because it is attached to something that already happens.

The intervention is to help each person attach one thing to one existing moment in their week. Week 6 does this deliberately. If you are running the boot camp differently, do it anyway.

# Part four: questions you will get and should answer carefully

## "Is this being tracked? Will my usage be reported?"

Find out the true answer before Week 1 and give it. If usage is visible to leadership, say so. If it is not, say so. If you do not know, say you will confirm by Friday and then confirm by Friday.

Hedging here costs you six weeks of credibility. People assume the worst when you are vague, and they are frequently right to.

## "Can I put member information into this?"

Follow your organization's guidance and do not improvise. What you can say without risk:

> "Not in this boot camp. Everything here is synthetic and that is deliberate, because practice should not carry risk. For live work, follow whatever guidance compliance has given, and if you are not sure whether something counts, ask before you paste rather than after."

If your organization has not published guidance yet, say that plainly and say who is working on it. Do not fill the gap with your own interpretation.

## "What if it gives someone wrong information and a member is harmed?"

> "The person who used it is responsible for what they did with it. That does not change. What changes is that the first draft arrives faster, which means the review step is now the whole job rather than most of the job. That is why we spend two weeks on verification before we spend a week on building anything."

## "Are you getting something out of running this?"

Somebody will ask, usually indirectly. Answer honestly.

> "Yes. I get to run a program and show it produced something, and that is good for me. I also think it is genuinely useful, which is why I picked this one instead of something easier. Both are true."

Defensiveness here reads as something to hide. A straight answer reads as somebody worth working with.

## "Why you? You are not in IT."

> "Because this is not an IT problem. It is a question about how we do our work, and the people who do the work should be the ones figuring out how it changes."

# Part five: things that go wrong in the room

## Somebody dominates the session

Give them a job. Ask them to demo in Week 3 or to be first in the Week 6 running order. People who talk a lot usually want to contribute and do not have a channel for it. Giving them one converts the problem into an asset.

If it continues, address it privately and directly. Do not manage it with facial expressions and hope.

## Nobody talks

Do not fill the silence. Count to eight before you rescue a question. Most facilitators wait about three seconds, which is not long enough for anybody to decide to speak.

If it persists, stop asking open questions to the group and call on people by name, gently, starting with someone you know is comfortable. Once two people have spoken, the room usually opens.

## The demo fails live

Best thing that can happen. Narrate it.

> "This is what I was talking about. Watch what I do now."

Then fix it live. A recovered failure teaches more than a smooth demo, and it demonstrates that you are actually using this rather than performing a rehearsed sequence.

## Somebody catches you being wrong

Say "you are right, I had that wrong." Then move on without a paragraph about it.

In a room of people who work with careful documentation for a living, how you handle being wrong is being watched more closely than anything you teach.

# Part six: what to do if the program is not working

Sometimes it is not working. Attendance is thin, submissions are low, and the energy is flat.

Options, in order of preference:

**Shrink it and go deeper.** Six engaged people who produce real capstones beat sixteen passive attendees. Say plainly that you are focusing on the people who want it. Nobody is offended by this and the quality goes up immediately.

**Change the format, not the content.** Move from a scheduled session to twenty minute working blocks where people bring their own task. This works well when the content is fine and the calendar is the problem.

**Stop and report honestly.** If it genuinely is not landing, ending it at week 4 with an honest write up of what did not work is a better outcome than dragging six weeks to a limp finish. It also produces something worth reading, and a facilitator who can say what failed gets believed about what worked.

What not to do: keep running sessions to empty rooms while telling yourself attendance will recover. It will not, and every low attendance week makes the next one harder.
