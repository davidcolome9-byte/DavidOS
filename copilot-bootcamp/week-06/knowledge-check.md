# Week 6 Knowledge Check: Capstone

Five questions. This one is less about recall and more about whether you know what makes work count. Takes about four minutes.

---

**Question 1**

Which capstone is stronger?

A. A workflow that saved four minutes a day, measured across four weeks, with a prompt two colleagues can reuse
B. A general improvement in how you approach your work, with no single workflow named
C. A one time project that went unusually well
D. A large claimed improvement with no artifact anyone else can use

---

**Question 2**

You did not measure your workflow before you started. What is the best way to produce a before number?

A. Estimate a percentage improvement that feels about right
B. Do the task the old way once, time it, and say it was timed once
C. Leave the number out entirely
D. Use a round figure like "cut it in half"

---

**Question 3**

Why is "what did not work" a required part of the presentation?

A. It fills time
B. It shows humility, which reads well
C. It is the most useful content for the room and it makes the rest of your claims believable
D. It is a formality

---

**Question 4**

Which habit is most likely to survive past the boot camp?

A. Using Copilot more often
B. Checking whether Copilot could help with each new task
C. Pasting the weekly numbers into a saved prompt every Friday when building the update
D. Exploring new Copilot features regularly

---

**Question 5**

Your improvement was small. What should you do?

A. Combine it with other small things to sound larger
B. Present it as it is, with the real number
C. Skip presenting and just submit the card
D. Present a different workflow that sounds more impressive

---
---

## Answer key

**1. A.** Four minutes a day, measured, with a reusable prompt.

Four minutes a day is about sixteen hours a year, which is real. More importantly, it is recurring, measured, and it produced something two other people can run. B and D cannot be acted on by anyone else. C is a story rather than evidence, because it does not repeat. Small and real beats large and vague, every time, in front of any audience worth presenting to.

**2. B.** Do it the old way once, time it, say it was timed once.

Honest methodology stated plainly is credible even when the sample is one. Option A is the failure mode that damages you most, because a percentage with nothing underneath is detectable by anybody who has run a process improvement, and once one number is doubted the rest of your presentation is discounted. Option C throws away real information you could reconstruct in ten minutes.

**3. C.** It is the most useful content and it makes everything else believable.

A presentation with no failures reads as a sales pitch, and experienced people discount it automatically. Naming a specific thing that did not work does two jobs at once: the room learns where the limits are, and every claim you did make becomes more credible because you have demonstrated you will say when something failed.

**4. C.** The Friday trigger attached to work you already do.

A, B, and D are intentions. Intentions decay in about nine days. C is attached to an existing event in your week, so the trigger fires whether or not you remember to be enthusiastic about it. When you decide what to keep from these six weeks, attach it to something already on your calendar.

**5. B.** Present it as it is.

Option A is how manufactured numbers get built, usually by people with no intention of exaggerating. Option D means presenting something you did not actually do. Option C removes the only real benefit of a showcase, which is that a colleague watches you and thinks "I could do that." A small honest improvement is the most repeatable thing anyone can watch, and repeatable is what you want.

---

## The one that matters

Question 4. Six weeks of training produces nothing durable unless something gets attached to a trigger you already have. Before you close the last session, decide which single thing you are keeping and which existing moment in your week it hangs from. Write both down.
