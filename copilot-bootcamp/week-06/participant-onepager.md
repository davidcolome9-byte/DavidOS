# Week 6: Capstone and Showcase

**One hour. Everyone presents. Print this or keep it open in a second window.**

## The idea in one sentence

Leave with one specific thing you can describe to your manager in sixty seconds.

Not "I learned about AI." Something like "the weekly update used to take forty minutes and now it takes twelve, and here is the prompt."

## What today is

Five minutes each. One question from the room after each presentation. Hard stop at five, and yes, you will get cut off if you run long, so practice once with a timer.

This is not a test. Nobody is grading you. The point is that the person watching you present is going to think "I could do that," and that is how anything from these six weeks survives past next month.

## Your five minutes

Five parts. Roughly one minute each.

**1. The workflow, in one sentence.**
What you do, when you do it, and who it is for. "Every Friday I write the weekly update for my manager."

**2. What it cost before, with a number.**
Time, rework, errors, escalations, anything countable. Estimates are fine if you say they are estimates.

**3. What you changed.**
Show the prompt or the agent. Actually show it on screen. This is the part people write down.

**4. What it costs now, with a number.**
Same measure as part 2. If it did not get better, say so, and say what you learned.

**5. What did not work.**
Required. Everybody has one. This is often the most useful minute of the five.

## What counts as a good capstone

Small and real beats large and vague.

Four minutes a day is sixteen hours a year. That is a real capstone. "It transformed how I work" is not, because nobody can act on it.

Good subjects:
- A recurring document you write
- A check step you added before submitting something
- A weekly or monthly report
- An agent you built that somebody else now uses
- Something you tried that failed, with a clear explanation of why

## Numbers: estimate, do not invent

Credible: "About twenty minutes down to about eight, based on the last four weeks."

Not credible: "Seventy percent efficiency improvement."

Anybody who has run a process improvement can tell the difference instantly, and the invented number costs you more credibility than having no number at all.

Say where your estimate came from. "I timed it twice" is a legitimate methodology at this scale.

## Present your failure

If your workflow got faster but worse, present that. If you built an agent nobody used, present that. If a habit lasted nine days, present that.

A failure you can explain is more useful to everyone in the room than a success you cannot. It also makes you look like somebody who evaluates their own work, which is a better look than a clean win.

## The evidence card

Due Friday. It is in the exercise file. It takes about fifteen minutes.

This is the real deliverable of the whole boot camp. Six months from now, in a conversation about what you have contributed, you will not remember these numbers. The card is you writing them down while they are still true.

Fill it in for yourself, not for anybody else.

## The one ask

Before you leave the call, name one person on the floor you will show one thing to in the next two weeks. Type the name in chat.

One person. One thing. Two weeks. That is the whole commitment.

## Prep before Tuesday

- Pick your workflow. Do not wait until Monday night.
- Find your before number, or make an honest estimate and note how you got it.
- Have your prompt or agent ready to show on screen, not described from memory.
- Run through it once out loud with a timer. Five minutes is shorter than it sounds.

## What survives after this

Habits with a trigger survive. Habits without one do not.

"I will use Copilot more" is dead in nine days. "Every Friday when I build the update, I paste the numbers into this prompt" survives, because it is attached to something you already do.

When you pick what to keep, attach it to something already in your week.
