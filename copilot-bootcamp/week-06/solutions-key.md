# Week 6 Solutions Key

Facilitator copy. There is no right answer this week, so this file gives you what a strong capstone looks like, the two failure modes to expect, and how to handle them without deflating anyone in a room where everyone is presenting.

## What you are actually assessing

Not the size of the improvement. Three things:

**Specificity.** Can they name the workflow, the measure, and the change without hedging.

**Honesty.** Does the presentation include something that did not work, and does the number sound like a number a person arrived at rather than one they wanted.

**Transferability.** Can somebody else use what they built, or does it only work for them.

A person who saved four minutes a day, measured it honestly, and produced a prompt two colleagues can run has done better work than someone claiming a transformation with nothing reusable behind it. Say that out loud early in the session, because otherwise people with modest results will apologize for them, and apologies are contagious.

## What a strong showcase looks like

The worked evidence card in the exercise file is the model. A strong showcase is that card delivered aloud in five minutes, with the prompt on screen at minute three.

Four things separate the good ones:

**The before number has a source.** "I timed it twice in the first month and got 24 and 27" beats any percentage.

**The artifact is on screen, not described.** People screenshot a prompt. Nobody screenshots a description of a prompt.

**The failure is specific.** "I told it to match our usual tone, which meant nothing to it" teaches the room something. "It took some trial and error" does not.

**It ends with a name.** Your closing question, "who else on the floor could use this tomorrow," puts a person in the room and attaches a small obligation to them. That question does more for adoption than the presentation did.

## Failure mode one: the transformation with no artifact

**What it looks like.** Someone presents with genuine enthusiasm. Copilot changed how they work. They are faster at everything, they think differently about their tasks, and they would recommend it to anyone.

Five minutes pass. There is no workflow named, no number, and nothing on screen anybody could use.

**Why it happens.** Sometimes it is true. Some people did genuinely change how they work in six weeks and cannot isolate one thing. More often, they did not prepare, and enthusiasm is what you present when you have nothing prepared. Both look identical from the front of the room, so do not try to diagnose it live.

**What it costs.** Nobody in the room can act on it. It also quietly damages the whole showcase: after two of these, the room stops expecting concrete content and starts treating the session as a feelings exercise. That is when your good presenters start trimming their own detail to match the register.

**The tell.** No number. No screen share. Sentences using "everything" and "how I think about."

**How to handle it live, without deflating them.** Do not challenge the claim. Ask the specific version of the question:

> "Pick the single one you would show a new hire on their first week. Just one. What is it?"

They will almost always produce something concrete, because they do have something, they just presented at the wrong altitude. That question rescues the slot and takes twenty seconds.

**How to handle it after.** Direct message, not a channel post. "You clearly got a lot out of this. Your card is going to matter more than your presentation did. Pick the one workflow and put a number on it, even a rough one." Most people deliver a strong card after that nudge.

**What to say to the room, once, early:** "I am going to ask everybody for one thing and one number. If you feel like everything changed, that is great, and I still want the one thing."

## Failure mode two: the number that was manufactured

**What it looks like.** A presentation with impressive figures. A 70 percent reduction in handle time on a task. Three hours a week saved. An 80 percent improvement in first draft quality.

Ask where the number came from and the answer is soft. "That is about what it feels like." Or worse: the before number was estimated after the fact by someone who wanted the improvement to be large.

**Why it happens.** Rarely dishonesty. Usually two things: the person is presenting to a room and wants their work to count, and nobody measured beforehand, so the before number gets reconstructed by someone who already knows how the story ends. That reconstruction drifts, reliably, in one direction.

**What it costs.** More than an inaccurate slide. If a participant repeats an inflated number to a manager and the manager repeats it upward, the correction lands on the participant. In a credit union, where several people in the room have spent careers being careful about what they assert, a manufactured number also costs them credibility with peers who can smell it.

And it costs you. If your program summary contains one number that does not hold up, everything else in it gets discounted.

**The tell.** Round percentages with nothing underneath. A before number that is exactly double the after. Any figure the presenter cannot explain the derivation of in one sentence.

**How to handle it live.** Never say the number sounds wrong. Ask the neutral question:

> "How did you land on the before number?"

If they have a real answer, you just gave them a chance to strengthen their case in front of everyone. If they do not, they will say "honestly, that is a rough estimate," and the room learns the standard without anyone being corrected. Either outcome is good, which is why this is the right question.

**How to handle it after.** In the evidence card, require the source line for every number. That requirement is already in the template and it does most of the work quietly. If a card comes back with a figure and no source, reply asking for one sentence about where it came from. People revise on their own once asked.

**What to say to the room, once, early:** "Estimates are fine. Say they are estimates. 'About twenty minutes down to about eight, timed twice' is credible and a clean percentage with nothing behind it is not, and everybody in this room can tell the difference."

## Handling the person who presents a real failure

Somebody will present something that did not work at all. An agent nobody used. A prompt slower than doing it manually. A habit that lasted nine days.

Give them more time than the successes, not less. Ask what they would do differently. Thank them specifically and by name.

Two reasons. It is genuinely the most useful content in the session, because the room learns where the limits are. And it determines whether next quarter's cohort tells you the truth. If honesty is rewarded visibly once, in front of everyone, people remember it.

## Consolidating what you collected

Within 48 hours, pull from the cards into the shared evidence tracker:

- Count of participants completing the boot camp
- Count of evidence cards submitted
- Total documented time savings, stated as a range, never as a single confident figure
- Count of reusable artifacts produced, meaning prompts and agent specs somebody else can run
- Count of agents built with named owners
- Prompts contributed to the shared library
- Three direct quotes, with names, used with permission
- The "what did not stick" list from the pattern round

That last item is the one most facilitators skip and it is the one that makes the rest believable. Anyone senior evaluating this program will trust a summary that names what failed considerably more than one that reports only wins.
