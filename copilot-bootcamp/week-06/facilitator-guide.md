# Week 6 Facilitator Guide: Capstone and Showcase

**Session length:** 60 minutes
**Format:** Teams meeting, everyone presents
**Prep time for you:** 60 minutes of session prep, plus 2 to 3 hours of one on ones. Ten minutes times fourteen people is not an hour. Block it now, and if you cannot protect three hours, run five minute calls with everyone rather than ten minute calls with half the cohort

## What you are trying to accomplish

Week 6 is not a celebration and it is not a review. It is the week where the work becomes evidence.

Three outcomes matter, in this order:

**One. Every participant leaves with one documented before and after.** Not a feeling that things are better. A specific workflow, with a specific old cost and a specific new cost, that they can describe in sixty seconds to their manager.

**Two. The group sees each other's work.** Adoption after a boot camp does not come from the boot camp. It comes from watching a peer do something useful and thinking "I could do that."

**Three. You leave with an evidence base.** You are facilitating this, which means at some point somebody will ask what it produced. The answer needs to be specific and it needs to come from artifacts you collected, not from memory.

Say all three out loud. The third one is yours and it is legitimate, and people can tell when a facilitator is collecting something they have not named. You disclosed it in Week 1. Do not go quiet about it now.

## Before you start

This is the week where the prep happens during the week, not the hour before.

1. **Run short one on ones on Monday and Tuesday.** Ten minutes each. Ask one question: "What are you presenting?" Anyone who does not have an answer needs help picking, right then, on that call. This single step is the difference between a good showcase and four people presenting while everyone else watches silently. [ASSUMPTION: you can find 10 minutes each with most of the cohort]
2. **Build the running order in advance.** Do not ask for volunteers on the day. Start with somebody solid, put your least confident presenter third or fourth rather than first or last, and end with your strongest.
3. **Enforce five minutes.** Have a timer visible. Announce that you will cut people off, then actually cut people off, kindly, on the first one. If you let the first person run to nine minutes, the last three people get two minutes each and learn that the schedule was a suggestion.
4. **Prepare the evidence card yourself.** Fill one in for your own facilitation work and show it as the demo. It models the format and it means you have your own record.
5. **Decide whether anyone outside the cohort attends.** A manager or two in the room raises the stakes usefully. It also makes some people freeze. If you invite guests, tell participants a week ahead. Never spring an audience on people.

## Run of show

### 0:00 to 0:05 Open

Keep it short. Everyone wants to get to the presenting or get it over with.

> "Six weeks. Here is the only thing I care about today: every one of you leaves with one specific thing you can describe to your manager in sixty seconds. Not 'I learned about AI.' Something like 'the weekly report used to take me forty minutes and now it takes twelve, and here is the prompt.' That sentence is worth something. Vague enthusiasm is worth nothing."

Explain the format: five minutes each, hard stop, one question from the room after each. Show the timer.

Then set the tone for what gets presented:

> "I am going to say this once. If your workflow got faster but worse, present that. If you built something and abandoned it, present that. A failure you can explain is more useful to everyone here than a success you cannot."

### 0:05 to 0:50 Showcases

A five minute slot costs 6.5 minutes in practice: five to present, thirty seconds for the screen share handoff, sixty seconds for the one question. Forty five minutes holds six presenters, not nine. Do the arithmetic before you promise anyone a slot.

| Cohort | Format |
|---|---|
| 6 or fewer | One session |
| 7 to 12 | Two sessions, six each |
| 13 or more | Three sessions, or 90 minutes |

Do not solve this by cutting to a three minute format. Participants rehearsed five parts at roughly a minute each and were told five minutes in three separate announcements. Cutting on the day means they drop the last two parts, and the last part is "what did not work," which you told them was required.

Decide before the session and announce it in the Week 6 invitation. [ASSUMPTION: you can get a second hour on the calendar for a cohort over six]

**The presentation structure participants were given:**

1. The workflow, in one sentence
2. What it cost before, with a number
3. What you changed
4. What it costs now, with a number
5. What did not work

**Your job during each one:**

Keep time visibly. Ask one question after each, chosen from this list:

- "What would you tell somebody trying this for the first time?"
- "What is the failure mode you would warn them about?"
- "Who else on the floor could use this tomorrow?"

That last question is the adoption engine. It puts a name in the room and creates a small obligation.

Do not evaluate. Do not say "great job" after each one, because it becomes noise by the fourth person and it means nothing by the eighth. Say something specific or say nothing and move on.

**When somebody presents a failure,** give it slightly more time than the successes. Ask what they would do differently. This is what makes the next cohort honest.

### 0:50 to 0:57 The pattern round

Stop presenting. Ask the room:

> "What showed up more than once today?"

Let them answer. You will hear things like: everybody shortened something, the biggest wins were in writing rather than analysis, several people built the same kind of check step, three people independently ran into the same problem.

Write what they say in the chat as they say it. Those observations are the most credible summary of what this program produced, because the participants generated them rather than you.

Then ask the harder question:

> "What did not stick? What did you try during these six weeks and stop doing?"

This one takes courage to ask and the answers are the most valuable thing in the session. People will name real things: an agent nobody used, a prompt that was faster to skip, a habit that lasted nine days. Write those down too. A facilitator who can say what did not work is a facilitator people believe about what did.

### 0:57 to 1:00 Close and the ask

Two things, then stop.

**The evidence card.** Everyone completes theirs by Friday. It is short, it is in the exercise file, and it is the actual deliverable of the whole boot camp. Say plainly why it matters:

> "This is not paperwork for me. Six months from now, when you are in a conversation about what you have contributed, you will not remember the numbers. This card is you writing them down while they are true."

**The one ask.** Each person names one person on the floor they will show one thing to in the next two weeks. Have them type the name in chat, right then, before they leave the call. Not a commitment to a program. One person, one thing, two weeks.

That chat log is your adoption plan and it took ninety seconds.

## Talking points you can lift directly

On why the numbers matter:

> "Nobody doubts that this helped. What people cannot do is act on 'it helped.' If you tell your manager the intake check cut your reopened claims from twelve a month to four, that is a thing she can repeat to her boss. Give people something they can repeat."

On honest numbers:

> "Estimate, do not invent. 'About twenty minutes down to about eight, measured across four weeks' is credible. 'Seventy percent efficiency gain' is not, and anybody who has run a process improvement will know it is not."

On what happens next:

> "The habits that survive are the ones with a trigger. 'I will use Copilot more' dies in nine days. 'Every Friday when I build the weekly update I paste the numbers into this prompt' survives, because it is attached to something you already do."

On presenting a failure:

> "Show me what did not work. I will give it more time than the wins, and I mean that."

## Anticipated questions and how to answer them

**"What if my improvement is small?"**
Small and real beats large and vague. Four minutes a day is sixteen hours a year. Present the small real one.

**"What if I do not have a number?"**
Estimate honestly and say it is an estimate. "It used to take most of an afternoon and now it takes about an hour" is a legitimate before and after. Say where the estimate came from.

**"Can I present something I built for personal use?"**
Yes, if the skill transfers. Say what transfers.

**"What happens after this?"**
Answer honestly. If there is a follow up plan, describe it. If there is not, say so and say what you would recommend: the prompt library stays and gets contributions, agents get owners and review dates, and someone runs a thirty minute check in at thirty and ninety days. Do not promise a program you have not been given.

**"Do I have to present?"**
Yes, and it is five minutes, and everyone else is in the same position. If somebody has genuine anxiety about it, offer the small group option or let them present to you separately. Do not make it a public negotiation.

## Failure modes for you, the facilitator

- **You skip the one on ones.** This is the failure that ruins Week 6. Without them, a third of the room arrives with nothing prepared, the showcase becomes four presentations and eight apologies, and the energy dies. The ten minute conversations are the session.
- **You let the first presenter run long.** Everything downstream degrades. Cut the first one at five minutes, warmly, and the rest will self correct.
- **You praise everything.** By the sixth "great job," people stop hearing you and start suspecting you are not paying attention. Ask a specific question instead.
- **You skip the "what did not stick" round for time.** It is the most valuable seven minutes of the hour. Cut a showcase slot before you cut this.
- **You end without the one person ask.** Adoption after Week 6 comes almost entirely from participants showing peers. Get the names in chat before anyone leaves.

## After the session, within 48 hours

1. Post the pattern round observations to the channel, in the participants' own words.
2. Collect the evidence cards and consolidate them into the shared evidence tracker.
3. Send each participant one direct message naming the specific thing they presented. Two sentences. This is what people remember about the program a year later.
4. Write down, for yourself: total attendance across six weeks, submission counts, agents built, prompts contributed to the library, and the three strongest quotes. Do it now while it is accurate.
5. Schedule the thirty day check in. Thirty minutes, optional attendance, one question: what is still in use. If you do not schedule it now, it will not happen.

## What to capture for your own records

Everything from the evidence cards, plus the "what did not stick" list. That second list is what separates a facilitator who ran a program from one who understands their team's adoption, and it is the more valuable of the two when somebody senior asks a hard question.
