# Week 4 Facilitator Guide: Copilot as Writing Coach

**Session length:** 60 minutes
**Format:** Teams meeting, one round of read aloud
**Prep time for you:** 45 minutes, plus a compliance conversation you should start a week early

## What you are trying to accomplish

This is the week with the highest stakes and the lowest perceived difficulty, which is a dangerous combination.

Everyone in the room already writes. They write case notes, coaching feedback, escalation summaries, and in some functions, things that go to members. They believe writing is the easy part. So the risk this week is not that people fail the exercise. The risk is that they succeed at producing polished text and stop noticing what the polish is hiding.

Three things have to land:

One. The same facts have to be written three different ways for three different readers, and getting that wrong is not a style problem, it is an accuracy problem. A member facing letter that includes internal reasoning about fraud patterns is a compliance issue, not a tone issue.

Two. Copilot makes weak writing sound confident. That is genuinely dangerous in a shop where letters and case notes carry weight. If somebody is not sure whether a claim was denied for insufficient evidence or for confirmed first party activity, Copilot will write a fluent sentence either way and neither of you will notice the difference.

Three. Editing your own draft down is a better use of the tool than generating a new one. Most people default to generation. Generation is the flashy use and editing is the valuable one.

## Before you start

1. Post the exercise file and the one pager in the channel thread the morning of the session, and paste the link again in the meeting chat at 0:00 and at 0:25.
2. Read the synthetic case file in the exercise closely. You need to know exactly what it does and does not establish, because the entire exercise turns on facts that are absent from it.
3. Write the three outputs yourself. Do not skip this. You will coach far better having felt where it gets hard, which is the member letter.
4. Confirm what your shop actually requires in a member facing adverse decision communication and whether anything in this exercise conflicts with it. If you are not sure, say so in the session rather than teaching something that turns out to be wrong. [ASSUMPTION: no formal template was provided to you for this boot camp]
5. Decide whether you want anyone reading aloud. I recommend yes and I recommend you go first.

## Run of show

### 0:00 to 0:05 Open

Open with the stakes, not the skill.

> "Everybody here writes. So today is not a writing class. Today is about the fact that Copilot makes uncertain writing sound certain, and in our work the difference between 'we could not substantiate the claim' and 'we determined the transactions were authorized' is the difference between a routine denial and a problem. Those two sentences sound equally professional. Only one of them might be true."

Then set the exercise in one line: same facts, three readers, three documents, and the hard part is what gets left out.

### 0:05 to 0:15 Teach: three moves

Only three. Do not add a fourth.

**One. Write for the reader, not the file.**

A supervisor summary answers "do I need to do anything." A case note answers "can a colleague pick this up cold in six months." A member letter answers "what happened to my money and what do I do now." The same facts serve all three, and the ordering, the length, and the vocabulary are completely different.

Ask the room which of the three is hardest. Someone will say the member letter. They are right, and the reason is that it requires deciding what the member does not need to know, which is a judgment call that a model cannot make for you.

**Two. Confidence language is a factual claim.**

Teach this pair explicitly, on screen, side by side:

> "We were unable to substantiate that the transactions were unauthorized."
> "We determined the transactions were authorized by you."

Those are different findings. The first says the evidence did not support the claim. The second accuses the member of filing a false claim. Copilot will produce either one with equal fluency and will often drift toward the second because it is a cleaner sentence.

Tell people to read every output hunting for verbs that assert more than the evidence supports. Determined, confirmed, established, verified. Each one is a claim about what you know.

**Three. Editing beats generating.**

Show the two prompt patterns:

Generation: "Write a letter explaining the denial."
Editing: "Here is my draft. Cut it to 150 words, keep every factual statement exactly as written, and flag anything I asserted that the case file does not support."

The second one keeps your judgment in the document and uses the model for what it is genuinely good at, which is compression and consistency checking.

**Anticipated question here:** "Can I use this for actual member letters?" Answer honestly based on your shop's guidance. What you can say without risk: use it to draft and to check, and have a human own every word that leaves the building.

### 0:15 to 0:25 Demo

Use the synthetic case file.

**Pass one.** Ask for a member letter with a plain prompt. Read the output aloud. It will be fluent, sympathetic, and will almost certainly contain at least one assertion the case file does not support. Find it live, out loud. This is the most valuable ten seconds of the session.

**Pass two.** Add the constraint: "Use only findings stated in the case file. Where the file does not establish something, do not assert it. Do not state or imply that the member authorized the transactions unless the file says so." Rerun. Compare.

**Pass three, the editing move.** Take your own draft, paste it, and ask: "Cut this to 150 words. Do not change any factual statement. List separately anything I claimed that is not supported by the case file above." Show the list it produces. That list is the actual product. The shortened letter is a bonus.

Narrate the decisions, not the features.

### 0:25 to 0:45 Hands on

Twenty minutes, working alone. Two outputs plus the audit. The case note is homework, and say so at 0:25 so nobody budgets session time for it. Three documents plus a self audit is a forty minute task and you have twenty.

Chat prompts to drop:

At 0:30: "If you are stuck on the member letter, write the supervisor summary first. It is easier and it clarifies what you actually know."

At 0:35: "Before you finish, read your member letter hunting for the words determined, confirmed, and verified. Check whether the case file supports each one."

At 0:40: "Word counts matter today. If your member letter is over 200 words, cut it rather than submitting it long."

At 0:43: two minute warning.

### 0:45 to 0:55 Debrief, with reading aloud

This is the week to read work out loud. Text that sounds fine on screen falls apart when spoken, and member letters get read aloud by members.

You go first. Read your own member letter. Then ask two participants to read theirs.

After each one, ask the room a single question: **"What did that letter claim that the case file does not establish?"**

Do not ask whether it was good. Do not ask for general feedback. That one question keeps the debrief specific and keeps it from becoming a critique of somebody's writing style, which will shut the room down fast.

Then ask the group the question that matters most: "What did you decide to leave out of the member letter, and why?" Omission decisions are where the real skill lives and people rarely get a chance to talk about them.

Close by naming the pattern: **same facts, different reader, and confidence is a claim.**

### 0:55 to 1:00 Submission and close

Restate submission. Preview Week 5: "Next week you stop writing prompts one at a time and build something that holds the instructions for you."

## Talking points you can lift directly

On why tone is not the point:

> "People think editing is about making writing sound better. In our work, editing is mostly about removing things that should not be in the document. That is not a style skill, it is a judgment skill, and it is the part nobody can do for you."

On the fraud angle:

> "In a case note, the facts and your conclusions have to be visibly separate. Six months from now, somebody picks up your note and has to be able to tell what you observed from what you concluded. If those two are blended into one nice paragraph, you have written a story instead of a record."

On fluency:

> "Copilot writes like someone who is always sure. Most of our real findings are not that clean. When the writing is more confident than the evidence, the writing is wrong even when every individual word is defensible."

On why members read letters differently than we write them:

> "Assume the member reads it twice, once for the number and once for anything they can argue with. You have all taken the second call. Write for that one."

## Anticipated questions and how to answer them

**"Is it okay to have Copilot write the whole letter?"**
Draft, yes. Send without a human reading every word, no. Somebody owns each sentence that leaves the building and it cannot be the tool.

**"How do I keep it from sounding robotic?"**
Give it a shorter word limit than feels comfortable. Robotic writing is usually padded writing. Also read it aloud. Anything you would not say out loud, cut.

**"What if the case file genuinely does not tell me why the claim was denied?"**
Then you cannot write the letter yet, and that is the correct finding. Write the supervisor summary saying what is missing. The exercise is built so this happens, and noticing it is the point.

**"Can I use this for coaching feedback to my team?"**
Yes, and it is one of the better uses. Give it the observations and the relationship, and tell it explicitly that this is peer coaching, not a performance action. Week 3's prompt 2 covered exactly this.

**"Does using this mean my writing gets worse over time?"**
It can, if you only generate. It does not if you draft first and use the tool to compress and check. That is why we spend time on the editing pattern today rather than only the generation pattern.

## Failure modes for you, the facilitator

- **You critique someone's writing style in the debrief.** The room closes immediately and you will not get volunteers again for the rest of the boot camp. Stay on the one question: what did it claim that the file does not establish.
- **You skip reading aloud because of time.** It is the highest value ten minutes of the session. Cut the teaching segment instead.
- **You let the member letter become the whole session.** The case note matters just as much for the fraud reviewers in the room, and it is the one that pays off in Week 6.
- **You give a confident answer about member communication requirements you have not verified.** If you do not know, say you will confirm. This is the week where a wrong answer from you could actually cause harm downstream.

## What to capture for your own records

Log every instance where a participant caught an unsupported assertion in their own output. Those are the most defensible outcomes in the entire program, because they demonstrate that the training produced a control rather than just adoption. Write down the specific claim that got caught.

Also collect the three outputs from two or three participants who did well. Sanitized examples of the same facts written three ways make an unusually good artifact when you need to show what this program produced.
