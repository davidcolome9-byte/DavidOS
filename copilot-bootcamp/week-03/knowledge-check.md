# Week 3 Knowledge Check: Prompt Coach

Five questions. Answer from memory first, then check yourself. Takes about four minutes.

---

**Question 1**

A teammate shows you a bad Copilot output and asks what they did wrong. What is the correct first move?

A. Rewrite their prompt for them
B. Ask which of the four elements is missing: goal, context, source, or shape
C. Tell them to add more detail
D. Suggest they try a different tool

---

**Question 2**

A prompt lists eight metrics by name, asks for a summary of all of them, and produces a thorough 900 word report that the manager does not read. What is the primary missing element?

A. Source, because it did not scope the data
B. Goal, because it did not say what a summary is for
C. Shape, because it did not say how the output would be consumed
D. Nothing is missing, the manager should read more carefully

---

**Question 3**

You rewrite a prompt and get an excellent result. Your partner runs the identical text and gets something aimed at the wrong audience. What is the most likely cause?

A. Your partner made a typing error
B. Copilot works better for some people than others
C. Your prompt relies on context that exists in your head but not in the text
D. The prompt needs to be longer

---

**Question 4**

Which rewrite actually improves a weak prompt?

A. Adding "please" and "thank you" and asking for a clear, helpful answer
B. Adding "write it the way we normally do these"
C. Adding "give me four bullets under 15 words each, written for an agent reading this on a live call"
D. Adding "be thorough and cover everything relevant"

---

**Question 5**

You want to add a prompt to the team library. What is the test it has to pass?

A. It produced a good result for you at least twice
B. It works for somebody who was not in the room when you wrote it
C. It is under eight lines
D. It uses all four elements

---
---

## Answer key

**1. B.** Ask which of the four is missing.

Option A is the tempting one and it is a trap. Rewriting it for them solves today's problem and teaches nothing, so they come back next week with the same question. The diagnostic question takes eight seconds and it transfers. Option C, "add more detail," is the advice that produced the 900 word report in question 2.

**2. C.** Shape.

This prompt is more detailed than most weak prompts and still fails, which is the point. Naming eight topics instructs the model to cover eight topics at equal weight. What was never stated is that this gets read on a phone in ninety seconds, so the correct output covers what changed and drops what did not. Detail is not the same as specification.

**3. C.** Context that exists in your head but not in the text.

This is the most common outcome of the partner swap and the most useful. You knew who the audience was, so the output looked right to you. Your partner did not, and neither did the model. A secondary contributor is that your work content and your partner's differ, so a prompt reaching your files reaches different material for them. Either way, the fix is to put the context in the text.

**4. C.** The bullets, the word limit, and the reading situation.

A is politeness, which changes tone and specifies nothing. B is the phrase that kills prompt libraries, because "the way we normally do these" is a note to yourself. D produces length, not usefulness. C names shape and context in one line and it is the shortest of the four options.

**5. B.** It works for somebody who was not in the room.

This is the only test that matters. A prompt can hit all four elements, run under eight lines, and work twice for you, and still fail cold for a coworker because it says "our usual format." Libraries die when three people try a contributed prompt, get mediocre results, and quietly stop opening the library. Test it on one other person before you contribute it.
